Cognitive Chimeras: The Dynamics of Category Extension in the Hierarchy of Concepts

People often use words in ways that are literally incorrect but pragmatically effective. Calling a friend a saint is not a theological claim but a way of highlighting their generosity or patience. In category extension, the lexical meaning of a term is stretched to include entities not covered by its literal denotation, allowing speakers to communicate rich information with minimal effort. Category extension also has legal consequences: when a brand name comes to stand for an entire type of goods, as with thermos, aspirin, or bubble wrap, it risks genericization and, with it, the loss of trademark protection.

Although category extension is a ubiquitous mechanism of efficient communication, its theoretical treatment remains fragmented. Research in Relevance Theory treats it as one kind of loose language use, alongside approximation, hyperbole, and metaphor, but what distinguishes it from these phenomena remains underexplored, and empirical data supporting this typology are lacking. The literature has focused heavily on proper names used as common nouns (an Einstein for a genius), the role of prototypes remains largely speculative, and we know little about how extended categories become lexicalized over time.

Research question

The project asks how and why speakers construct unlexicalized categories that deviate from a community’s shared conceptual taxonomy. It pursues five interlocking objectives:

  1. establishing an empirically grounded, multidimensional typology of loose language use and situating category extension within it;
  2. determining how idealization (“the new Roger Federer”) and demonization (“a real Judas”) of individuals influence the use of proper names as common nouns;
  3. examining how efficient communication through category extension becomes established and standardized in a community’s shared lexicon;
  4. analyzing how speakers’ emotions, values, and goals drive loose categorization; and
  5. operationalizing and measuring the genericization of brand labels using computational corpus-linguistic tools.

Methods

The project combines computational corpus analysis, survey experiments, and conceptual analysis, so that its theoretical claims are tested against natural language data. Beyond advancing our understanding of how we adapt language to cognitive and communicative demands, it aims to give linguists, cognitive scientists, and legal scholars tools to predict, analyze, and mitigate the real-world consequences of category extension.
